# Building a Networked Organization: Restructuring the IT Department at MWH (A) — Notes

📄 Case PDF: [[Building_a_Networked_Organization_MWH_A.pdf]]

## Quick Read
> Darden case (Rob Cross et al.). **Vic Gulas**, a 20-year veteran of engineering-consulting firm **MWH** (global leader in water/hydropower/environmental remediation; >$975M revenue, >6,000 staff), has been made head of IT and must convert a geographically organized department into a **functionally organized global unit ("iNet")** — 185 people, 27 offices, 11 countries — delivering standardized service at much lower cost, live by 2004. Success depends entirely on collaboration among people who have never met. Gulas runs an **organizational network analysis (ONA)** — a survey asking "whom do you turn to for information?" (90% response) — to get a baseline "X-ray" of who is and isn't connected. It reveals a network only **6% connected**, fragmented by geography rather than function, with the two strategic new offshore centers (Christchurch NZ, Pune India) the *least* connected. The question: what specific interventions should Gulas prioritize to build the *right* connectivity before go-live?

## Case Notes

### Key facts
- **The transformation:** old = geographic silos (6 email systems, 3 ERP systems, "tribal," "cowboys," IT cost above industry benchmark, slow). New **iNet** (Exhibit 1) = 3 functional areas — **Global Service Provider** (messaging/network/support; commodity work moved to low-cost **Christchurch, NZ**), **Solution Development** (enterprise apps; new **Pune, India "Resource Net"**), **Strategy & Administration** (direction, PMO, fee administration) — plus 3 regional directors. 12 posts moved to NZ (4 people relocated); no offices closed.
- **ONA method:** at an IBM knowledge-management conference Gulas learned ONA — ask "Whom do you turn to for help? Whom do you trust? Who inspires you?" and map responses into node-and-line pictures. He surveyed all 185 iNet staff (Aug 2003, 10 days, **90% response**) to get a birth-state "X-ray."
- **Findings:**
  - **Fragmented by geography, not function:** only **6%** of possible ties exist; clustered within locations; European/American/Asian teams separated; **Christchurch and Pune — the strategic centers — have the lowest collaboration with everyone** (Exhibits 2–3).
  - **Walnut Creek** has the highest *internal* connectivity (48%) but low external ties — an inward, self-reliant group not on board with the reorg.
  - **Denver ↔ Pasadena** app-dev groups barely collaborate though they serve the same customers — duplicated work (HR built a workflow the Pasadena ERP already had).
  - **Expertise groups** under-connected; Server/Network/Desktop map held together by a few US "glue" people (Exhibit 4) — fragile, hard to assimilate newcomers.
  - **Hierarchy is not the constraint** — people reach *up* for info — but **front-line and junior staff are under-connected** (Exhibit 5), the people who most need to share expertise and who leave if isolated.
  - **Network size (Exhibit 6):** avg 9 ties; huge range — some people have 1–2, others are **overloaded with 25+ dependents** (one pulled 3 all-nighters) → bottleneck / key-person risk. **Louise Martin** is highly central (expert + mentor, "sat in" for a director) but won't take a management role in iNet.
  - **Peripheral people** (e.g., Kathy Davidson, sole ERP support in Chicago, boss/peers gone post-merger) are less satisfied and more likely to leave.
  - **Information biases (Exhibit 7):** people name mostly their immediate team, few ties outside MWH (is new industry/tech info entering?); most interactions are unplanned/serendipitous (people stick to a familiar subset rather than seek the best expertise).
  - **Gulas's own network (Exhibit 8):** of 7 people he relies on, 4 are in Denver and 4 are directors — too narrow.

### Central decision
- **What specific interventions should Gulas prioritize** to build the *right* (not maximal) connectivity before the 2004 go-live: which gaps to close first (geographic fragmentation; isolated Christchurch and Pune; Denver–Pasadena duplication; disconnected front-line and expertise groups), which existing ties and key people (central experts, brokers) to protect or reposition, how to integrate peripheral people and the new offshore centers, and how to relieve over-reliance on a few overloaded individuals?

### Key takeaways
- **Read the map for roles, not just density:** central connectors/experts (Martin), boundary-spanning brokers, isolates/peripherals (Davidson), overloaded bottlenecks, and cohesive-but-inward subgroups (Walnut Creek) — none of which the org chart shows.
- **Match connectivity to strategy:** 100% connectivity is a costly drag; build only the ties the design requires — and the ONA shows iNet's current pattern (6%, geography-based, isolated offshore centers) is exactly what the functional-global model must overcome.
- **Structure alone won't do it:** reporting-line changes must be paired with targeted connection-building — staffing rotations, shared projects, named expertise-group leaders, deliberate onboarding for Pune/NZ, linking Denver–Pasadena to kill duplication — plus attention to incentives and the "US dominance" cultural perception.
- **Manage the network's health:** codify knowledge and spread load off overloaded experts; give central people a mentor/architect/community role that uses their ties without a director title; pull peripherals in via new supervisors and cross-site work; watch front-line/junior connectivity for retention.
- **What the leader should do:** don't mandate blanket "more communication" or buy tools reflexively — triage the most strategically costly gaps, protect the ties that work, invest in a few high-leverage connectors and boundary spanners, import outside information, re-run the ONA to measure progress, and widen his own Denver/director-heavy personal network.

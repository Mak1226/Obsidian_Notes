# Houston, We Have a Problem: NASA and Open Innovation (A) — Notes

📄 Case PDF: [[Houston_We_Have_a_Problem_NASA_and_Open_Innovation_A.pdf]]

## Quick Read
> HBS case (Tushman, Lifshitz-Assaf, Herman). January 2011. After a 2005 budget cut slashed 45% off the R&D budget of NASA's **Space Life Sciences Directorate (SLSD)** at Johnson Space Center, director **Jeff Davis** and strategist **Elizabeth Richard** spent years seeding **open innovation** — broadcasting well-defined problems to outside solver crowds via platforms like InnoCentive, yet2.com and TopCoder. A fall-2009 round of pilot challenges produced spectacular results (a semi-retired engineer in New Hampshire cracked solar-flare prediction that had stumped NASA for decades). At the January 2011 workshop meant to convert skeptics, ~60 SLSD scientists and engineers **rejected the results as "so what?" / "not relevant to our work"** — "a lead balloon crashing with a deep thud." Davis must figure out why the reaction was so negative and what to do to get open innovation adopted into day-to-day research.

## Case Notes

### Key facts
- **The burning platform:** 2005 cuts took SLSD R&D from ~$330M to $175M (−13 FTEs, ~−80 contractor posts). Davis and Richard's strategic response (from 2006) was to sustain R&D volume through **more external collaboration and alliances** despite a smaller budget.
- **Who SLSD is:** ~1,000 professionals (**~80% contractors**, mostly Wyle); NASA controls all budget/procurement. Mission = keep astronauts healthy on the space frontier. A **deep-expertise, high-specialization, clear-hierarchy** organization in a small field where everyone has known each other for decades; members see themselves as "the brains behind the vehicles" ("Actually, it *is* rocket science"). Since the early 1990s the culture has become **safety-obsessed and risk-averse** — "you almost hate to have a new idea, all the people you'll then have to talk to."
- **Building toward open innovation (2006–2010):** visioning retreat; benchmarking ~15–20 orgs (finding: **100% pursued alliances because they *had* to collaborate to innovate**); an Innovation Lecture Series; a Threadless (T-shirt crowdsourcing) workshop that skeptics couldn't relate to spaceflight; an HR-led culture-change day scientists rated "low priority." Karim Lakhani (Harvard) connected Davis to challenge platforms.
- **The pilots (fall 2009 – 2010):** 12 challenges from SLSD knowledge/tech gaps run on **InnoCentive** (7), **yet2.com** (6) and **TopCoder** (1), plus **NASA@work** (internal crowdsourcing across all 10 centers). **>2,800 solvers from >80 countries.** The **solar-flare prediction** challenge: winning solution 85% accurate at an 8-hour horizon (vs. NASA's 1–2 hours) — from a **semi-retired radio-frequency engineer**; national press, US CTO shout-out. TopCoder's medical-kit packing algorithm was folded into NASA's Integrated Medical Model. Follow-ons: the **Harvard-NASA Tournament Lab** and the **NASA Human Health and Performance Center**. Davis's instinct: **keep open innovation integrated inside the directorate**, not spun out. Context worsened mid-stream: the **Constellation program was cancelled (2010)** — layoffs, "no time for this innovation thing… what does it do for me?"
- **The rejection (Jan 2011 workshop):** when Davis moved from "we experimented" to "let's integrate this into your day-to-day research," reactions were largely negative and emotional:
  - **Confidentiality:** "Isn't there a problem with using this and us being NASA?"
  - **False confidence:** a null result on one platform doesn't mean no solution exists — "InnoCentive isn't the whole world… not the golden bullet."
  - **Identity threat (the core):** "We're NASA. We are the only ones who know space. And we already innovate." "To reach out to others to solve it — it's like cheating [against the scientific method]." "They are the ones… there isn't anyone who knows better what they need." Using outside solvers implies the researcher is no longer *the* expert and loses control of the problem and its solution.
  - **Extra unfunded work:** "additional work" on gaps and challenges while already under-resourced; seen as "Jeff's pet project."
  - Davis: "The not-invented-here syndrome was strong… How could the reactions be so negative, and what should we do?"

### Central decision
- **What should Davis do to get open innovation adopted as a normal part of SLSD research** after the pilots' success produced a "so what?" rejection — how to reframe it so it complements rather than threatens the experts' professional identity, quality standards and control, and what organizational changes (roles, incentives, process, structure; integrated vs. separate) will make it stick?

### Key takeaways
- **Fit of open innovation to problem type:** it excelled at **well-defined, broadcastable, algorithmic/computational or materials** problems with clear solution criteria (solar-flare prediction, medical-kit packing, food-packaging material) where diverse outside expertise helps; it is ill-suited to tacit, deeply contextual, integrated-systems work and never replaces the internal experts who must *frame* the problem, *evaluate* solutions and *integrate* them.
- **The barrier is professional identity, not logic:** SLSD scientists define themselves as *the* problem-solvers on life-or-death problems; asking a crowd (including non-experts) to solve "their" problem reads as an admission of incompetence, a status/control loss, even "cheating." That is why the benchmark evidence didn't move them.
- **Other resistance:** mission-sensitivity/IP fears; the "false-confidence" worry; a safety-obsessed, form-heavy, risk-averse culture; and acute budget/Constellation stress making anything extra feel like unfunded survival tax. Plus champion fatigue ("Jeff's pet project").
- **Reframe:** position open innovation as a **complement that expands what in-house experts can do** — the expert still owns the problem, sets criteria, judges and integrates; it frees experts from routine problem-solving for the hard integrative work; deploy it **selectively** for the right class of problems.
- **Make it stick organizationally:** give it legitimacy and resources (dedicated roles/office, kept **integrated** in the directorate); build the skill (writing challenges, setting criteria, evaluating submissions); change incentives/metrics so using external solvers is rewarded, not penalized as "not your own work"; secure visible senior sponsorship beyond Davis to de-personalize it; start with willing early adopters and let wins spread; and remove the real constraints (protected time, funding, clear IP/confidentiality rules).
- **Deeper lesson:** adopting an outside-in innovation model inside an expert, mission-driven, high-reliability bureaucracy is an **identity and culture change**, not a process rollout — results are necessary but nowhere near sufficient.

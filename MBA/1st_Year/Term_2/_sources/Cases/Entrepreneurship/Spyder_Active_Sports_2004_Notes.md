# Spyder Active Sports—2004 — Notes

📄 Case PDF: [[Spyder_Active_Sports_2004.pdf]]

## Quick Read
> HBS case. Spring 2004. **David Jacobs** (age 70), founder/CEO/Chairman of **Spyder Active Sports** — a premium technical ski-apparel brand he started in Boulder in 1978 — has spent seven years partnered with Denver private-equity firm **CHB Capital Partners**, during which gross sales grew from ~$10M to ~$61M. CHB now needs a liquidity event (its 7-year hold has "well exceeded its expected term"), and Jacobs, thinking about retirement, wants to harvest value. IPO is off the table (Spyder is small and highly seasonal). The class task: **what is Spyder worth, and how should Jacobs structure the sale of his and CHB's equity** — a trade sale to a strategic buyer, a secondary sale to another PE firm, or keeping control and selling only CHB out — balancing price, his own liquidity/semi-retirement, and the futures of his sons (Bill, Jake) and key managers.

## Case Notes

### Key facts
- **Founder history (theme: insist on ≥50% ownership):** ex–Canadian downhill champion and national-team coach; serial entrepreneur with a rocky record (Lange boot recall; Jacobs Corp sold under financing pressure). Spyder started as a mail-order maker of premium "technical" ski sweaters; reconstituted 1982 as Spyder Active Sports with Japanese contract manufacturer **Tsunehisa Shimokubo** (50/50 by 1986).
- **Pre-CHB plateau (1995):** stuck at $10–15M sales, no growth strategy; Jacobs ~65, wanting to secure his finances after years of reinvesting; fashion brands (Hilfiger, Nautica, Prada, Armani) eyeing "his turf." Advisors said: raise a bigger capital base — **sell the whole company or take a minority partner** ("selling 51% = might as well sell it all"). Robertson Stephens ran a 1997 process; Nike flirted, then chose snowboarding/hockey.
- **CHB deal (Sept 1997):** CHB ("Closely Held Businesses") does low-volume, high-touch minority partnerships ("we compete with our expertise, not our money"; "$100k-a-week McKinsey team"). Due diligence found forecasts below the offering memo → CHB proposed a **sliding-scale stake tied to EBITDA targets** (35% at $20.5M cumulative EBITDA, 60% if <$10.5M). **Jacobs rejected the sliding scale**, took a fixed deal: **$4.5M in two stages** → CHB ends at **37.9%** fully diluted; employees (incl. Jake) 11.3%; Jacobs and Shimokubo 25.4% each.
- **What CHB's 7 years added (the growth story being sold):** quarterly working board; an ERP "built for a $400M brand, not a $10M brand" (J.D. Edwards); a marketing-driven global brand manager, **John Walbrecht** (ex-Doc Martens), and "spend ahead of revenue" events (a $150k Vail dealer event vs. a prior $25k ceiling); industry-data-driven strategy (sell-through, category share, price points); in-store **concept shops**; a **brand architecture across price points and channels** with strict "channel cleanliness" (Vail specialty shops vs. Macy's must not overlap); and a shift from distributors to **direct selling** in Europe.
- **Financials (Exhibit 5, $000, FY ends Mar 31):** Net sales FY01 27,303 → FY03 40,290 → **FY04 61,366** (unaudited); forecast FY05E 85,281 → **FY08E 165,732**. EBITDA FY01 1,327 → **FY04 9,582**; forecast → FY08E 27,895. Net income FY04 5,069 (was −$972k in 2000 during heavy investment). FY04 balance sheet (Exhibit 6): total assets $15.9M, cash $8.0M, equity $6.5M.
- **Seasonality:** FY04 monthly sales ranged ~$22M (June) to near zero (Jan 2003); working-capital debt averaged ~$8M, ranged $0–$23.4M. ⇒ too small and seasonal for an IPO without first getting much larger and multi-season.
- **Exit setup:** CHB + Jacobs engaged **Financo** (retail) and **Wachovia Securities** (consumer products; banker Joe Pellegrini) for a total or partial sale. Comparables in Exhibits 9 (transactions), 10–11 (public companies).

### Central decision
- **What is Spyder worth in the open market, and how should Jacobs structure the sale of his (and CHB's) stake** — (a) trade sale to a **strategic buyer**, (b) secondary private placement to a **financial/PE buyer**, or (c) keep a **controlling stake** and let CHB sell only its minority — to best balance maximum price, his own liquidity and semi-retirement, the roles/futures of Bill and Jake and key managers, and the company he built?

### Key takeaways
- **Valuation:** apply comparable-transaction and comparable-company multiples (EV/EBITDA, EV/sales; Exhibits 9–11) to FY04 EBITDA (~$9.6M, or a normalized figure) and DCF the aggressive forecast (sales $61M→$166M, EBITDA $9.6M→$27.9M by FY08). The **gap between trailing results and the hockey-stick forecast is the negotiation zone**: a strategic buyer pays for growth + synergies; a financial buyer discounts it. Adjust for seasonality and the thin balance sheet.
- **Strategic vs. financial buyer:** a strategic (larger apparel/outdoor group) likely pays the **highest price** (distribution, synergies, portfolio) but may absorb the brand and replace management; a PE buyer keeps Spyder independent with management in place but pays a lower, return-driven price and just **resets the same exit clock**.
- **Founder's shifting objective:** at 45 and 65 Jacobs fought for control (rejected subordinate roles, rejected CHB's sliding scale); at 70, "being fairly paid for decades of effort" now outweighs steering the company — the classic **control → harvest** shift.
- **One deal can't serve every goal:** liquidity, top price, brand independence, and guaranteed roles for Bill, Jake and Walbrecht are partly in tension — rank them, and use tools (partial sale, earn-out, retained minority, negotiated employment/lock-ups for key people) to bridge gaps.
- **Succession is a value driver:** if Jake (product) and Walbrecht (marketing) are "the creative engine," retaining and incentivizing them is part of getting the deal done at a good price.

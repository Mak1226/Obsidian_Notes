# The Fish Whisperer — Notes

📄 Case PDF: [[The_Fish_Whisperer.pdf]]

## Quick Read
> Ivey case (Jim Kayalar). **Brody Conner**, a 48-year-old Alaskan car mechanic — no college, two failed repair shops, two failed marriages, an avid poker player who doesn't separate business money from his own — is a gifted amateur rod-maker and fisherman ("think like a fish to catch a fish"). His hand-built **Catch-All Rod** (one rod for all fish up to 60 lb, in all conditions) sold well by word of mouth, so he started **Catch-All Rods (CAR)** to sell year-round beyond seasonal Alaska. Across four years he bootstraps on ~$5,250, is refused bank loans (bad credit, prior bankruptcy), struggles with cash flow, fulfillment and customization-vs-scale, takes on investor **Mike Haas** (25% equity) and then ends up in court with him over financial disclosure and patent ownership. By Year 4 he is "making a decent living but not striking it rich" and must choose: bring in a new investor to scale, hire a manager, or sell the company and live off royalties.

## Case Notes

### Key facts
- **Founder profile:** skilled craftsman + strong personal salesmanship + real fishing insight; but finance-illiterate (told the accountant to minimize tax; mixes personal and company funds), volatile temper, heavy drinker/gambler, unreliable at work, poor credit + prior personal bankruptcy, health issues, age 48.
- **Product/positioning (Exhibit 1):** the Catch-All Rod replaces multiple rods; retail **$375**, retailer ~$300, **wholesaler $200**, **COGS to CAR ~$100**. Aimed at the largely unsegmented freshwater market + 25% of saltwater.
- **Market:** ~30–40M US anglers; US rod market ~$1.12B (2006); fragmented — local makers, mass merchandisers, and cheap imports (China, Japan, Europe); no side-by-side rod test has ever been done. 10–11% quarterly federal excise tax on fishing gear.
- **Year 1:** underestimated setup costs; self-built website got **no traffic**; sold via pickup-truck trips, samples and consignment; library research → list of 100 wholesalers; wholesalers demanded samples, terms, promo pricing, consignment he couldn't fund; pricing method unresolved; banks said no; negative cash flow; AR problems (late payers on 60-day terms), overdrafts, fulfillment errors (wrong buyers, over/under-shipments, returns). Survived but exhausted; kept the mechanic job.
- **Year 2:** demand outran build-to-order capacity; considered China OEM; took investor **Mike Haas** (asked $125k → $50k; Haas wanted 40%, Conner offered 15%; **settled at 25%**, plus a paid-for business plan). Pre-bought raw materials, batch-made common parts — but most rods need **hand customization**, so scale stalled. Admin hire quit in peak season; used temp-visa students. Conner resented Haas's reporting/profit demands. CAR grew **20%**.
- **Year 3:** Haas put in a further **$25k for a utility patent** (North America; filed in Conner's name as inventor). Dispute: Haas said the patent belonged to the JV; Conner "rented" it to the JV for a fee + royalties, then set up a **Delaware LLC**, sold CAR's majority shares to it and licensed the patent to it. Haas **sued** over undisclosed financials and misrepresenting patent ownership. Grew **15%**.
- **Year 4:** tired of managing; explored China OEM (FOB/CIF, customs broker, LC — bank unhelpful); guerrilla/social marketing still drew little traffic; started reselling Chinese-made rods from local distributors on his site.
- **Exhibit 1 business plan:** projects **550,000 rods / 8.85% of the US rod market** over three years — Year 1 alone 137,500 units, **$27.5M revenue, $13.75M profit** — for a one-man shed operation. Funding sought: **$125,000**.

### Central decision
- **What should Conner do with CAR now** — take a new investor and try to scale, hire a professional manager to run it, or sell the company and live off royalty payments? Underneath: **was CAR ever a real opportunity, and is Conner the right founder to build it** given his skills, temperament, finances, health and age?

### Key takeaways
- **Founder–opportunity fit is the binding constraint:** the rod may be genuinely good, but Conner's finance illiteracy, inability to scale, temperament, money habits and health make *him* the limiting factor — the classic "great craftsman, wrong CEO."
- **Customization vs. scale:** an artisanal, hand-tuned product resists mass production; every growth lever (batch parts, hiring, offshore OEM) fights the thing that made the product special.
- **Under-capitalized bootstrapping:** ~$5,250, no bank access, consignment + 60-day terms + late payers + fulfillment errors = a permanent cash crunch; textbook small-business working-capital and operations failure.
- **Sanity-check top-down forecasts:** an 8.85% national share and $25M first-year revenue for one person is a fantasy — investors heavily discount founder projections for good reason.
- **Govern the investor relationship and the IP:** mismatched valuation expectations, then fights over reporting, distributions and patent ownership; "renting" the patent to the JV and the Delaware-LLC move are self-dealing that invited litigation. Assign IP cleanly and treat a co-owner fairly.
- **Realistic endgame:** professionalize with a manager + controls + clean IP, or **license/sell the design to an established manufacturer and take royalties** (fits his temperament) — not the $50M growth story.

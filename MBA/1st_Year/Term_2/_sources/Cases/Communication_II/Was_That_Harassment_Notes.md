# Was That Harassment? (HBR Case Study) — Notes

📄 Case PDF: [[Was_That_Harassment.pdf]]

## Quick Read
> Fictional HBR case (J. Neil Bearden, INSEAD). At Coltra, a global beverage company, salesman **Jackson Pierce** jokes to a new colleague, **Teaira**, that she was picked for the high-potential leadership program "because of your good looks, too." A third colleague, **Rainer Wolfson**, who half-started the banter, feels obliged to report it to HR (**Suzanne Bibb**) under Coltra's zero-tolerance sexual-misconduct policy. HR classifies the remark as "highly offensive" but not "egregious," and now the choice sits with Teaira: **should she push the formal complaint forward against Jackson?** The case is a vehicle for debating intent vs. impact, bystander duty, proportionality of zero-tolerance policies, and how to have the follow-up conversations.

## Case Notes

### Key facts
- **Setting:** kickoff conference call for Coltra's 2019 leadership cohort (~50 salespeople globally); Houston office. Jackson is a solid but not top-tier performer; Teaira has been "crushing it"; Rainer is a high performer who transferred from Munich, took an MBA at ESMT Berlin.
- **The remark:** Jackson and Rainer were ribbing each other ("must've been my good looks"); Teaira walked in mid-conversation, Rainer took the phone off mute, and Jackson said "I guess you're here because of your good looks, too, Teaira." She and Rainer did not smile.
- **Escalation path:** Rainer stewed, called a friend in Berlin, then reported to HR the same afternoon — partly to not "stand by." He worries it will be "blown out of proportion" and knows Teaira will know it was him (he was the only other person there).
- **Policy context:** CEO Peter Mackenzie announced a **zero-tolerance policy** a year earlier; all staff did harassment training. HR has a defined spectrum (evident misconduct / egregious / highly offensive). HR has seen a post-#MeToo spike in complaints.
- **Teaira's read:** initially "it really wasn't a big deal"; but Jackson has competed with her since day one — cuts her off in meetings, has taken credit for her ideas; she doesn't fully trust him. His later "explanation" was a defense, not an apology.
- **Structural backdrop:** Coltra's entire C-suite is men except the CHRO; only one female board member. Top salesperson for years running was a woman (Ying).
- **Marginal "classroom notes" (1–9)** flag research on ambiguous promotion criteria, leaders' stated positions shaping harassment concern, and the argument that zero-tolerance can deter reporting.

### Central decision
- **Should Teaira formally push the complaint forward against Jackson** — knowing it could get him fired under zero-tolerance — or handle it informally (talk to Jackson directly, accept his explanation, ask HR for a lesser "unprofessional behavior" track)?
- Sub-questions: What does Rainer owe as a bystander, and did reporting without asking Teaira first take away her agency? How should HR run the conversations with Jackson, Teaira and the managers? Is a single ambiguous joke a fireable offense?

### Key takeaways
- **Intent vs. impact:** a comment can be "meaningless joke" to the speaker and still undermine and demean; the standard the case pushes is how it lands and whether it reflects/reinforces bias, not just what was meant.
- **Zero-tolerance is a blunt instrument:** it signals seriousness but removes proportionality and can discourage people from reporting minor incidents for fear of disproportionate consequences.
- **Bystander action requires the target's voice:** Rainer's instinct to report was defensible, but doing it without first talking to Teaira converted her incident into a process she didn't choose.
- **Process design matters:** clear tiers of severity, informal resolution options, and protecting the complainant's choice about disclosure and escalation are what make a policy workable rather than feared.
- **Likely class split:** proportionalists (informal chat + coaching for Jackson) vs. those who argue that letting it slide is exactly how a male-dominated culture stays that way.

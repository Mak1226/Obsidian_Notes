# Rough Waters Brewing Company: Evaluating Decisions in Uncertain Environments — Notes

📄 Case PDF: [[Rough_Waters_Brewing_Company.pdf]]

## Quick Read
> Ivey case. Fall 2020. Partners **Jennie O'Keefe** and **Chris Johnson** own **Rough Waters Brewing Company (RWBC)**, a values-driven ("beer with purpose, business for good") craft brewery in **Deer Lake** on Newfoundland's sparsely populated, tourism-dependent west coast, launched Nov 2019 — four months before COVID-19 shut the province. One year in, they face a crowded and growing NL craft-beer market, a weak provincial economy, provincial-monopoly pricing (the NLC sets one price per product and takes a cut on off-site sales), and no access to federal payroll supports. They have several growth options they can't pursue all at once — switch bottles → cans (and upgrade label design), expand off-site distribution, invest further in the new taproom, professionalize brand management, pursue **B Corp certification** — and little time, money or information. The task: how to evaluate and sequence these decisions rigorously under deep uncertainty rather than keep improvising.

## Case Notes

### Key facts
- **Positioning trade-off already made:** located on the west coast (Deer Lake, pop. ~4,600) near NL's only west-coast airport and the highway to Gros Morne (~233,000 visitors/yr) — **less competition but highly seasonal and tourism-dependent**, and customers must be drawn to a remote spot.
- **Industry:** ~19 NL craft breweries, ~300 employees, market of ~0.5M people; a 2016–2019 "craft beer boom" now crowding and intensifying; brewers are **cooperative, not cutthroat** ("we don't want to fight over 2% of the market").
- **NLC (provincial retailer/regulator):** sets a single province-wide price (RWBC beer ~$5.50); RWBC earns **less** through NLC/convenience channels (distribution fees), **more** in-house via the taproom. Fall 2020 tax cut raised a sub-100,000 L brewer's take on a $4.00 can from ~$2.70 to ~$3.15; still taxed higher than the rest of Canada.
- **Economy:** NL population 521,364 and declining; unemployment 12.3% → 14.1% (2019→2020); highest debt/capita in Canada; but high household alcohol spend ($1,029 vs. $757 rest of Canada).
- **COVID support gap:** federal wage subsidies required a payroll; RWBC (opened December, no employees) didn't qualify — relying on MP/MHA relationships.
- **Product:** four staples in 500 mL bottles (Sou'wester Sour, Selkie Wheat, Scallywag IPA, Signaler Porter) + limited runs; already **award-winning** (Down East Brewing Awards) — quality is proven.
- **Decision options on the table:**
  1. **Bottles → cans:** consumers prefer cans (handling, eco, stackable, convenience-store friendly, no taste difference), but a canning line costs "tens of thousands," and RWBC's hand-drawn tattoo-artist labels are a **distinctive brand asset** that switching could erode.
  2. **Sales channels:** off-site bottled distribution was the original plan (production-first location, no taproom); it accidentally helped during COVID, but competitors (e.g., Crooked Feeder) expanded canning + off-site capacity; craft shelves are crowding.
  3. **Taproom:** not in the original plan; funded by a spring 2020 Indiegogo campaign and self-built. Higher margin, direct engagement, builds craft culture — but constrained by COVID occupancy limits, tourist seasonality, no nearby food, and a hard space trade-off: **on bottling days the taproom must close** (same room).
  4. **B Corp certification:** ~$1,100/yr + a lengthy process; aligns with their values, but unknown whether customers would notice or whether it lifts sales.
  5. **More formal, professional brand management** vs. their current "highly informal," improvised style.
- **Exhibit 1:** comparison of ~25 NL microbreweries (year, region, price range, packaging, channels, online ordering, food offerings).

### Central decision
- **Which strategic move(s) should O'Keefe and Johnson prioritize for growth** — canning + label redesign, distribution expansion, deeper taproom investment, professional brand management, B Corp — given severe constraints on time, money and information? And more fundamentally, **what disciplined decision process** lets them evaluate options under deep uncertainty instead of continuing to improvise?

### Key takeaways
- **Frame the decision explicitly:** list the options, the key uncertainties (competitive intensity, tourism recovery, provincial economy, whether tax margins actually improve, consumer response to cans and to B Corp), the binding constraints (one small space; a lumpy canning-machine spend; founders' time), and the interdependencies (taproom vs. bottling share the room; cans unlock convenience-store distribution).
- **Separate controllable from uncontrollable:** packaging, channels, certification and brand management are choices; the pandemic, the economy and NLC pricing/tax are context to plan *around* with scenarios.
- **Preserve optionality (real options / affordable loss):** the canning line is a large, partly irreversible bet — sequence investments, run cheap tests first (test-market cans; survey customers on labels and on B Corp) to buy down uncertainty before committing.
- **Brand vs. efficiency tension:** distinctive hand-made bottle labels stand out on a crowded shelf, but cans + professional design are what the channel and consumers increasingly reward — changing packaging risks year-one brand equity.
- **Purpose as identity, not a growth lever:** the case is explicit that social-cause marketing doesn't reliably move beer sales; treat "people, planet, profit" and B Corp as authenticity, not a guaranteed sales driver.
- **Structural constraint = seasonality/remoteness:** any growth plan must reconcile ambition with a location that depends on tourists and on making people come to you (echoes the winter-tourism problem in the Cold Opportunity cases).

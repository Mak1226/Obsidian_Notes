# Kalamazoo Zoo — Notes

📄 Case PDF: [[Kalamazoo_Zoo.pdf]]

## Quick Read
> HKS case (Linda Bilmes). March 2010. **Rory Lyons**, director of the city-run **Kalamazoo Zoo** (Michigan), gets the FY2009 (year ending Dec 31, 2009) financials and finds large variances he suspects are weather-driven (record-wet winter → more closed days; unusually warm spring → an animal-breeding boom). The city faces state-aid cuts; the Mayor wants a presentation on the zoo's financial health and a preliminary next-year budget. The exercise: perform **revenue and expenditure variance analysis**, then decompose ticket revenue and animal-food spending into **price and quantity variances**, say what else Rory should investigate, and evaluate three options for closing a further **$100,000 state-subsidy cut**.

## Case Notes

### The 2009 numbers (Table 1, $)
- **Total revenues:** actual **850,000** vs. budget **820,000** → **$30,000 Favorable** — *but* gate revenue −$20,000 U and individual donations −$50,000 U; the surplus is entirely extra **restricted conservation grants (+$50,000)** and **state subsidy (+$50,000, a one-off renovation grant)**. Core earned revenue actually fell.
- **Total expenditures:** actual **1,070,000** vs. budget **820,000** → **$250,000 Unfavorable**. Overruns: **animal food +$120,000**, **overtime +$60,000**, **utilities +$20,000**, **visitor transportation/facilities +$50,000**. All staff salary/wage/fringe lines exactly on budget.
- **Bottom line:** budgeted to break even; actual = **$220,000 deficit** (850,000 − 1,070,000).

### Operating data (Table 2) and price/quantity variances

**Ticket revenue** — actual 10,000 visitors × $10 = $100,000; budget 15,000 × $8 = $120,000 → total **$20,000 U**.
- **Quantity variance** = (10,000 − 15,000) × $8 = **$40,000 Unfavorable**
- **Price variance** = ($10 − $8) × 10,000 = **$20,000 Favorable**
- *Read:* the zoo raised price 25% but lost 33% of visitors (partly the 230 vs. 250 open days; partly price sensitivity) — the price hike did **not** offset lost volume; gate revenue is down.

**Animal-food expenditure** — actual 120 animals × $3,000 = $360,000; budget 100 × $2,400 = $240,000 → total **$120,000 U**.
- **Quantity/usage variance** = (120 − 100) × $2,400 = **$48,000 Unfavorable** (20 extra animals from the breeding boom)
- **Price/rate variance** = ($3,000 − $2,400) × 120 = **$72,000 Unfavorable** (food cost per animal up 25%)
- *Read:* the overspend is **more price than volume** — it isn't just "we have more animals"; per-animal feeding cost rose sharply (inflation? species mix? spoilage?).

### What else Rory should investigate (Q4)
- **Overtime** ($434/day actual vs. $120/day budget over 230 days ≈ $100k): storm response, extra animals, understaffing? Need hours and headcount.
- **Food price jump** (+25% per animal): supplier contracts, species mix, waste — need per-species/per-supplier detail.
- **Utilities** (+67%): weather-driven consumption vs. rate increase.
- **Visitor transportation/facilities** doubled ($50k→$100k) *despite fewer visitors* — why?
- **Donations**: 1,000 donors averaging $50 vs. 500 averaging $200 — why did large donors pull back?
- Confirm the **conservation grants are restricted** (can't cover the deficit).
- Is 2009 a **weather anomaly or a trend**? Need multi-year data and a proper fixed-vs-variable cost model; identify which costs scale with animals, open days and visitors.

### Options for a further $100,000 shortfall (Q5) — net budgetary impact vs. *actual* 2009
| Option | Calculation | Year-1 impact | Ongoing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Raise ticket price to $15** (visitors −20% → 8,000) | 8,000 × $15 = $120,000 vs. actual $100,000 | **+$20,000** | +$20,000 |
| **2. Rehome 20 animals → 100** ($1,000 transport each) | food saving 20 × $3,000 = $60,000; one-time cost 20 × $1,000 = $20,000 | **+$40,000** | **+$60,000** |
| **3. Cut one of two assistant zookeepers** | salary $50,000 saved; severance 10% × $50,000 = $5,000; 6 months fringe ≈ $9,850 (fringe ≈ 39% of salary) | **≈ +$35,000** | **≈ +$70,000** (salary + fringe) |

- **No single option closes the $100,000 gap in year 1.** A combination is needed (e.g., 2 + 1, or 3 + 1). Steady-state, option 2 or 3 alone gets close to $60–70k.
- Non-financial costs: option 1 hurts access/attendance for a public zoo and risks more demand loss; option 2 shrinks the collection and may conflict with conservation-grant conditions; option 3 leaves one keeper for 120 animals (service/safety risk; note keepers ≠ animal handlers).

### Central decision
- **Diagnose the zoo's 2009 performance with variance analysis** — a $220,000 deficit driven mostly by cost overruns (food $120k = $48k volume + $72k price; overtime $60k; utilities $20k; visitor facilities $50k) while core earned revenue fell and the apparent revenue surplus is restricted money — then advise Rory on what to investigate and how to close a further $100,000 state-aid cut.

### Key takeaways
- **A favorable total-revenue variance can hide a revenue problem:** always break totals into controllable/earned vs. restricted/one-off components — here gate and donations fell $70k, masked by grants.
- **Decompose every big cost variance into price × quantity:** the food overrun is mostly a *price* problem (per-animal cost +25%), not just the breeding boom — different problems need different fixes.
- **A static budget mixes volume effects with efficiency effects:** the zoo needs a *flexible* budget (per open day, per animal, per visitor) to judge management performance against weather-driven volume changes.
- **In public budgeting, restricted funds don't rescue an operating deficit** — confirm fund restrictions before counting grant money as relief.
- **Cost control beats the cut menu:** fixing overtime, food pricing and visitor-facility spend would recover far more than the $20–70k each of the three options yields — investigate the overruns first.

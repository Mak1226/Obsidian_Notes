# Dabur India: Growing Professional Management from Family Roots — Notes

📄 Case PDF: [[Dabur_India_Growing_Professional_Management_from_Family_Roots.pdf]]

## Quick Read
> HBS case (Srinivasan, Arora, Goldberg). December 2018. **Sunil Duggal**, CEO of **Dabur India** (founded 1884 as an Ayurvedic medicine maker; by 2018 a ~$1.09B-revenue FMCG major, ~$8B market cap, 250-product portfolio, largest herbal-remedy producer in the world), plans to retire and must ensure a clean handoff to the next CEO. Dabur professionalized in the mid-1990s — the Burman family (still ~68% owners, 4 board seats) separated ownership from management and left headquarters — but a botched first attempt (1998–2001) sent the stock from INR 175 to INR 46 and produced the company's first-ever loss, before Duggal (CEO from 2002) rebuilt it over 16 years. The successor must embrace Dabur's evidence-based-Ayurveda values, know the sprawling portfolio, and sustain the delicate high-trust equilibrium with the family.

## Case Notes

### Key facts
- **The family model outgrown:** by the mid-1990s Dabur was a 5th-generation business with **8 family members in senior roles**, owning 79% of stock. Problems: cross-generation and cross-branch disagreement, "you can't fire family members when they don't perform," and an executive suite lacking modern FMCG expertise (against HUL, Colgate, P&G, Godrej; later Patanjali).
- **McKinsey's prompt (mid-1990s):** most family firms don't survive three generations; "just because your last name is Burman, does that mean you are the best managers in the world?" → **separate ownership from management, hire the best person for each job.** Done from strength, not crisis — "a healthy ship… the family decided to use that as an opportunity."
- **Mechanisms of professionalization:**
  - 1997–99 the **entire executive suite replaced with outside MNC hires** (new CEO, CFO, etc.).
  - The **family physically left HQ** (built an office 50 km away) so "people had to know we would not be around."
  - Family **exited all executive roles** and later **gave up all salaries and perks** — the Family Council amended its constitution to remove the claim — to signal commitment and reassure investors.
  - Board professionalized: 12 directors = **4 non-executive family + 2 executive + 6 independent**; 4 family seats split equally across the two Burman branches; chairmanship rotates by consensus; independent directors oversee all major decisions.
  - **Family Council (2000)** — all male family members over 25 — plus a **family constitution** (dividend, succession); over time a forum for updates. **P.D. Narang** (non-family CA, joined 1979) is the intermediary between the Management Committee, the Board and the Family Council.
  - **Decision rights:** CEO can act alone up to **INR 100M** financial impact; above that, the Board. Share sales require **right of first refusal to other family members** (keeps ownership concentrated).
- **The failed first attempt (1998–2001):** first professional CEO (ex-Colgate/P&G) cleared out long-tenured managers fast → churn in the top ranks + G.C. Burman's death (2001) → decision quality collapsed just as the family withdrew → **stock INR 175 → 46, first annual loss ever**, "spiraling out of control." Family resumed hands-on control; the outside management was let go almost wholesale; company run by the family for months.
- **Duggal's rebuild (CEO from April 2002):** joined 1995 from PepsiCo, rose via the personal-care business; "cleaned up the sins of the past" with big P&L hits year one; recruited a new senior team himself ("hungry for growth"); created a **Management Committee** ("the CEO is not omniscient"); four-year roadmaps, quarterly budget reviews, SOPs, ESOP. Results: crossed $1B turnover 2011–12; market cap INR 17B (2001) → INR 170B (2010); "every four years the company doubles its market cap." Strategic shifts: international expansion (6 → 17 overseas plants; Namaste/Hobi acquisitions), brand-building and diversification (Balsara, Fem; 14 brands >INR 1B by 2015), e-procurement and the "DARE" retail-segmentation program, all-natural/chemical-free repositioning.
- **How the family–professional balance is held:** transparent, good-faith engagement (family sits in on business reviews, hears good and bad, is kept in the loop); management is candid even when challenging the family; family gives input on M&A, unbudgeted capex and big external shocks (demonetization, GST) but **management decides**. Illustrations of the boundary working both ways: the Hajmola variant a family director disliked ("Sir, you are not the target audience" → big success) vs. the Binaca toothpaste the family wanted piloted but marketing declined (too many toothpaste brands). Family cannot issue checks, place orders, hire, or buy equipment — "they cannot interfere."

### Central decision
- **How should Duggal and the Board choose and onboard the next CEO** so the "professional management from family roots" model endures — a leader who embraces Dabur's evidence-based-Ayurveda values, knows the 250-product portfolio, and can maintain the high-trust equilibrium with the Burman family that a disastrous first attempt and a 16-year rebuild established?

### Key takeaways
- **Why separate ownership from management:** third-generation survival odds, conflict among many family members in senior roles, inability to hold relatives accountable, and a need for professional FMCG capability. Doing it from strength makes it a choice, not a rescue.
- **What made it stick the second time:** a family constitution and Family Council to channel the family's voice; the family leaving HQ *and* executive roles *and* salaries; a board dominated by independent/executive directors with rotating chairmanship; a trusted intermediary; precise decision rights; and concentrated ownership via right of first refusal.
- **Why the first attempt failed:** a clean sweep of long-tenured managers destroyed continuity and capability transfer exactly as the family withdrew — professionalization needs staged withdrawal, not a purge.
- **Keeping values and long-term orientation with professionals:** high-trust transparency plus professionals who stay "sensitive to Dabur's values"; the family is an engaged owner, not a manager.
- **Lessons for family firms:** professionalize before you must; make it real ("not in name only") to attract talent; build formal family governance; define decision rights; align incentives (no family salaries, ESOPs); treat the relationship as a two-way accommodation sustained by trust and results.
- **The open question:** succession is the stress test — the handoff must preserve values, portfolio knowledge, and above all the family–professional trust equilibrium.

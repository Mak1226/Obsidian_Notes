# When the Tone of an Email Went Wrong — Notes

📄 Case PDF: [[When_the_Tone_of_an_Email_Went_Wrong.pdf]]

## Quick Read
> Ivey case set at the fictional Premier School of Management, New Delhi (2015). Professor **Pradeep Sharma** accidentally emails an exam question paper to a student–alumni **Google Group** instead of the office. Alumna **Meeta Singh** replies-all: "I would suggest that you refrain from using Google groups to circulate test papers." In a high-power-distance Indian academic culture, students read this as disrespectful; Sharma's reply-all correction is read by others as sarcastic and condescending (he also corrects her grammar). A student, **Manish Kumar**, privately sends Singh a hostile message ("filthy language"). Singh apologizes repeatedly, then asks to be removed from the group and cuts ties with the school. The class task: diagnose exactly where tone, channel and audience turned a trivial admin slip into a relationship rupture, and rewrite the messages.

## Case Notes

### Key facts
- **Trigger:** 31 Mar 2015, Sharma sends the end-of-term PG exam paper to the wrong address (a group used by students, alumni and faculty), realizes only when the office calls; he has a backup paper ready. Confidentiality of the exam is compromised for everyone on the list.
- **Singh's reply (Email 2, reply-all, 1 Apr):** brief, imperative — "I would suggest that you refrain from using Google groups…" Intended as helpful/matter-of-fact; received as a junior publicly instructing a revered senior.
- **Sharma's reply (Email 3, reply-all):** explains it was a one-off mistake, notes others also flagged it, then adds "the expression … is not polite for a student to teacher communication," and signs "Thanks, anyway." Later (Email 5) he corrects her usage of "alum" vs "alumnus."
- **Singh's follow-ups (Emails 4 & 6):** "I am an alum and not a student… my profound apologies" — repeated apologies; ultimately asks to be taken off the group.
- **Kumar's private email (2 Apr):** "Even if you are not a student, you should not use such filthy language with a teacher… This is not your business to give suggestions to teachers." Escalates a public-etiquette dispute into a personal attack.
- **Character context:** Sharma (~55) is beloved, a mentor figure, has heart disease and admitted memory lapses. Singh is bright, blunt, task-focused, was preparing for a government exam and kept to herself. Kumar is seen as abrupt, conservative, somewhat isolated.
- **Cultural frame:** in Indian society professors hold "revered positions"; etiquette says students don't confront them — the sender's brevity/directness collided with that norm on a large, mixed, public list.
- **Fallout risk (Closing):** students fear an escalating "verbal duel" on the group, alienation of alumni, and a students-vs-alumni rift.

### Central decision
- **How should this have been communicated — and how should it now be repaired?** Where precisely did intent and interpretation diverge (word choice, imperative mood, reply-all vs private, public correction, grammar policing)? Rewrite Singh's message and Sharma's response. What is the right channel and audience for each, and who (professor, students, admin) should now act to keep Singh from leaving?

### Key takeaways
- **Channel + audience shape tone as much as words:** a private one-line note is helpful; the same words reply-all to a mixed hierarchy of hundreds read as a public rebuke.
- **Brevity reads as curtness in writing:** email strips prosody and facial cues, so terse, imperative phrasing ("refrain from…") is easily amplified into hostility, especially across a power gap.
- **Never correct someone publicly (or pedantically):** Sharma's "not polite" line and the "alum/alumnus" correction, on reply-all, made him look sarcastic and turned bystanders into combatants.
- **De-escalation beats being right:** every party had a defensible point; the damage came from litigating it on a broadcast channel instead of taking it offline early.
- **Practical rule-set:** match channel to sensitivity; assume wide forwarding; soften directives with framing and questions; acknowledge the other person's constraints; move to a call the moment tone becomes the topic.
